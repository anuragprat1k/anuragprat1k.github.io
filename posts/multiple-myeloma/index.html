In June of 2026, my father was diagnosed with multiple myeloma. This is a notebook of me trying to understand the disease from the ground up, in the order the sections below follow: the basics of cell biology, what goes wrong to turn a healthy cell cancerous, how myeloma is diagnosed, how it is treated, and how we know those treatments work — the clinical trials behind the current standard of care, with a note on how access to it differs between India and the US.

Basics of cell biology#

Cancer originates from a malfunction in the cell biology. So it is important to understand the basic machinery of the cell first, crudely its form and function.

Form — DNA, the blueprint#

DNA is the blueprint for the cell. Housed in the nucleus as a double helix, it is a long molecule consisting of two strands twisted together held by rungs between them and written in a 4-letter alphabet (A, T, G, C) that holds the instructions for the cell’s functions.

Each group of 3 DNA letters, called a codon, specifies one amino acid (read out later via transcription and translation); a stretch of DNA that codes for one protein is called a gene; and a long DNA strand holding many genes is packaged into a chromosome. A single chromosome holds anywhere from 50 million to 250 million letters. Humans have 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs — one of each pair inherited from each parent, so we carry two copies of every gene. One complete set of 23 chromosomes is called the genome (about 3 billion letters); each cell carries two copies of it, one from each parent.

A gene is defined by its position on the chromosome and the protein it codes for — not by its exact letters. The two copies we inherit sit at the same location and produce the same protein, but they need not be letter-for-letter identical: each parent may pass on a slightly different spelling of the gene (these versions are called alleles). Many such differences are harmless — the genetic code is redundant, so some letter changes don’t alter the protein at all, and others swap in a near-equivalent amino acid. This is why two people carry “the same gene” while differing in its exact sequence, and it is the basis of normal human variation. The rarer letter changes that actually corrupt a gene’s function — rather than leaving it intact — are what we call harmful mutations, and distinguishing the two is central to understanding cancer.

Function#

If DNA is the blueprint, proteins are the workers that actually build and run the cell. The instructions in the DNA exist to create proteins, and nearly everything a cell does is done by a protein of some kind.

Getting from blueprint to worker takes two steps:

  1. Transcription — a gene is copied from the DNA into a portable messenger molecule called mRNA. The master DNA stays safe in the nucleus; only the copy travels out.
  2. Translation — a machine called the ribosome reads the mRNA three letters (one codon) at a time, and for each codon adds the matching amino acid to a growing chain.

The finished chain of amino acids then folds into a specific three-dimensional shape, and that shape is the protein’s function. A protein shaped to speed up a chemical reaction is an enzyme; one shaped to carry oxygen is haemoglobin; one shaped to sit on the cell surface and receive signals is a receptor; one shaped to recognise invaders is an antibody.

One group of proteins deserves special mention: transcription factors, which bind DNA and switch specific genes on or off. This is how a single genome — identical in every cell — produces hundreds of different cell types. Every cell carries the same ~20,000 genes, but each cell type reads only the subset it needs: a muscle cell runs its contraction genes, a nerve cell its signalling genes, an antibody-producing plasma cell its antibody genes. A cell’s identity is essentially the particular pattern of genes it keeps switched on.

In short: DNA stores the instructions, proteins carry them out, and transcription factors decide which instructions each cell uses. This control layer — which genes are read, and how cell growth and division are regulated — is exactly what breaks down in cancer.

The cell cycle — how cells divide#

Cells multiply by dividing, and because every new cell needs a complete copy of the DNA, division is a carefully ordered process called the cell cycle. It runs in four phases: the cell grows (G1), copies its entire DNA (S), prepares and checks the copy (G2), and finally splits into two identical daughter cells (M).

Copying the DNA is also where much of the danger begins. Duplicating the cell’s entire DNA during the S phase is astonishingly accurate but not perfect — a few miscopied letters slip through on each division. DNA is damaged from the outside as well: ultraviolet light, tobacco smoke, chemicals, radiation, and even the ordinary byproducts of the cell’s own metabolism all leave marks on it. Any lasting change to the DNA sequence is a mutation.

Most mutations are harmless — they land in unused stretches of DNA, or (as with the alleles earlier) swap a letter without changing the protein — and dedicated DNA-repair systems fix much of the rest. But with trillions of cells dividing over and over across a lifetime, mutations inevitably accumulate with age, which is a large part of why cancer — myeloma included — is mostly a disease of later life. The rare mutation that lands in a gene controlling growth or death is the dangerous one.

What makes the cycle safe is a set of checkpoints between the phases — quality-control gates that inspect the cell before letting it proceed. If the DNA is damaged, a checkpoint either pauses the cycle for repair or, if the damage is beyond repair, triggers apoptosis — programmed self-destruction, so a faulty cell dies rather than passing its errors on. A key guardian of these checkpoints is a protein called p53, which halts division or orders apoptosis when it detects damage.

Two kinds of gene govern this balance: genes that promote division, acting like an accelerator, and genes that restrain it or trigger apoptosis, acting like a brake. In a healthy cell the accelerator and brake are balanced — it divides only when needed, and damaged cells are removed.

What goes wrong in myeloma#

Cancer is what happens when this control breaks down. When those accumulating mutations strike the genes that regulate division and death, the balance tips: a mutation that jams the accelerator on (these genes are called oncogenes) together with one that disables the brake (tumour suppressors, such as p53) produces a cell that divides without restraint and refuses to die. And because each division copies the damage forward, one corrupted cell becomes a growing population of them.

Myeloma is this process occurring in a specific cell type: the plasma cell, the body’s antibody factory. Normally, plasma cells live in the bone marrow and produce antibodies to fight infection. In myeloma, a plasma cell’s controls are corrupted, and it multiplies uncontrollably — with four main consequences:

  • It floods the blood with one useless antibody. A plasma cell’s job is to make antibody, so the cancerous clone mass-produces a single defective one, called the M-protein (measurable in the blood, and one of the main markers of disease burden).
  • It injures the kidneys. The clone sheds excess light-chain fragments that the kidneys must filter, and these can clog and scar them — so impaired kidney function is a common, and sometimes urgent, way myeloma first shows up.
  • It crowds out the bone marrow. As myeloma cells fill the marrow, they leave less room for normal blood production — causing anaemia (low red cells), low neutrophils (weak immunity), and low platelets.
  • It damages the bones. Myeloma cells disrupt the balance of bone maintenance, dissolving bone and causing lesions, pain, and fractures.

What makes myeloma so difficult to eliminate is that it is not one uniform tumour but an evolving population. The cells keep mutating, producing many genetically distinct sub-populations (subclones). Any single treatment kills the cells vulnerable to it but spares any resistant variant, which then regrows — so the cancer adapts to whatever is used against it. This is why myeloma is treated not with one drug but with several at once, and why it is currently considered treatable but not reliably curable.

Diagnosis#

Confirming myeloma, once it is suspected, comes down to catching the malignant plasma cells and reading their DNA. Two findings anchor the diagnosis, and both fall straight out of the biology above: the M-protein — the single antibody the plasma-cell clone floods into the blood — is measured as the disease’s fingerprint, and a bone marrow biopsy samples the marrow to confirm and count the clonal plasma cells. But the tests that lean most directly on the cell biology are the two that read the tumour’s genome from that marrow sample.

  • FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation) looks for the large-scale chromosomal abnormalities that drive myeloma — translocations that fuse two chromosomes (such as t(4;14) or t(14;16)), the loss of a chromosome region (del(17p), which deletes the TP53 gene behind p53), or extra copies of the long arm of chromosome 1 (gain/amp 1q). These are the same kinds of large mutations introduced earlier, and they sort patients into standard- and high-risk disease.
  • NGS (next-generation sequencing) goes finer, reading the actual letter-level mutations across a panel of genes — the point mutations that FISH is too coarse to see. It increasingly helps guide treatment, and the same sequencing technology underlies MRD (minimal residual disease) testing, which can later find a single myeloma cell among a million to measure how deep a remission truly is.

Together these turn the question from “is this myeloma?” into “which myeloma is this?” — the genetic profile that shapes how aggressive the disease is likely to be, and which treatments it will need.

Treatment#

Myeloma is treatable but, for now, not reliably curable — so the aim is not a single knockout blow but the deepest, longest remission possible. Because the disease is an evolving population of subclones (from the last section), no single drug holds it for long: the cells vulnerable to one agent die, and a resistant variant regrows. Modern treatment answers this in two ways — it hits the cancer with several drugs at once, each working through a different mechanism, and it is delivered in phases that press the disease down as far as it will go and then hold it there.

The toolkit#

Four classes of drug form the backbone of frontline treatment, and each exploits a different weakness — several of them the very features that make a plasma cell a plasma cell.

  • Proteasome inhibitors (e.g. bortezomib, brand name Velcade). A plasma cell is a protein factory, churning out antibody; to survive that workload it leans heavily on the proteasome, the cell’s protein-recycling machine that clears misfolded protein. Block the proteasome and the garbage piles up until the cell poisons itself — a vulnerability that myeloma’s own overproduction makes especially acute.
  • Immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) (e.g. lenalidomide, Revlimid). These act through a protein called cereblon to tag for destruction the transcription factors (Ikaros and Aiolos) that myeloma cells depend on, while also revving up the immune system’s T and NK cells.
  • Anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies (e.g. daratumumab). CD38 sits abundantly on the surface of plasma cells; daratumumab is itself a lab-made antibody that latches onto it and flags the myeloma cell for the immune system to destroy.
  • Corticosteroids (e.g. dexamethasone), directly toxic to myeloma cells and a long-standing partner to all of the above.

The later immunotherapies work differently again — instead of poisoning the cell directly, they recruit the patient’s own T cells to kill it, either by flagging a surface marker or by physically dragging a T cell onto the tumour. The diagram below shows where each of these treatments strikes.

First line: Dara-VRd and a stem-cell transplant#

For a newly diagnosed patient who is fit enough, the current standard of care is built around a quadruplet and a transplant, in four phases:

  1. Induction — Dara-VRd. The first months combine all four classes: Daratumumab + Velcade (bortezomib) + Revlimid (lenalidomide) + low-dose dexamethasone. Until recently the standard was the triplet VRd; adding daratumumab (the “quadruplet,” established in the GRIFFIN and PERSEUS trials) drives deeper responses and keeps the disease down longer, and has become the preferred induction where it is available.
  2. Autologous stem-cell transplant (ASCT). Once induction has knocked the disease back, the patient’s own blood stem cells are collected and frozen. They are then given a very high dose of melphalan — a chemotherapy strong enough to kill myeloma far more thoroughly than standard doses, but which would otherwise permanently wipe out the bone marrow. The saved stem cells are re-infused to rebuild blood production. (“Autologous” simply means the cells are the patient’s own.) A transplant does not cure myeloma, but it deepens the response and lengthens remission.
  3. Consolidation. A short further course to push the response deeper still.
  4. Maintenance. A gentler long-term therapy — usually lenalidomide, increasingly with daratumumab — taken for years to hold the remission in place.

The yardstick throughout is response depth, measured by MRD testing that can find a single myeloma cell among a hundred thousand or a million. Reaching MRD-negativity is the goal, because the deeper and cleaner the remission, the longer it tends to last.

Patients who are older or too frail for a transplant skip the ASCT step and are treated with a gentler ongoing combination instead — commonly daratumumab with lenalidomide and dexamethasone (Dara-Rd) — with the same aim of a deep, durable response.

When it comes back: later lines and the immunotherapy wave#

Almost all myeloma eventually relapses. Each time it does, the strategy is to switch to drugs the cancer has not yet learned to evade — rotating in a different proteasome inhibitor (carfilzomib), a stronger IMiD (pomalidomide), a different anti-CD38 antibody (isatuximab), or an agent aimed at specific genetics (venetoclax, where the cells carry the t(11;14) translocation).

The biggest recent change, though, is a wave of immunotherapy that turns the patient’s own T cells against the myeloma. Most of it targets BCMA, a protein found almost exclusively on plasma cells, with a newer alternative target called GPRC5D. Two formats matter:

  • CAR-T cells (e.g. ide-cel / Abecma and cilta-cel / Carvykti). A patient’s T cells are collected and genetically engineered in a lab to carry a receptor that recognises BCMA, multiplied, and re-infused as a “living drug” that hunts the cancer. A single infusion can produce remarkably deep and durable remissions. The trade-offs: manufacturing takes weeks (the patient often needs “bridging” therapy in the meantime), and the treatment can trigger cytokine release syndrome (CRS) — a systemic inflammatory reaction — and neurotoxicity. First used only at late relapse, CAR-T is now moving earlier in the sequence.
  • Bispecific antibodies (e.g. teclistamab and elranatamab, targeting BCMA; talquetamab, targeting GPRC5D). These are off-the-shelf antibodies with two arms: one grabs the myeloma cell (via BCMA or GPRC5D), the other grabs CD3 on a T cell, physically clamping the two together so the T cell kills the cancer cell. Unlike CAR-T they need no manufacturing wait and are given as ongoing dosing, but they too can cause CRS and raise the risk of infection.

Between them, CAR-T and bispecifics are a genuine answer to myeloma’s moving-target problem: they attack through an entirely different route from chemotherapy, and having two antigens to aim at (BCMA and GPRC5D) means that when the cancer escapes one, another is waiting.

A note on access (India vs the US)#

The frontline regimen — Dara-VRd, transplant, and lenalidomide maintenance — is broadly the global standard, and is available at major Indian centres. Much of the backbone is in fact relatively affordable there — India is a major producer of generic lenalidomide and bortezomib — so the real cost barriers are the still-patented daratumumab and the transplant itself. The sharpest India–US divergence is in the newest immunotherapies: CAR-T and bispecifics are widely (if very expensively) available in the US, while in India they are only beginning to arrive, through a handful of centres, clinical trials, and emerging local manufacturing. Which lines are used, and in what order, is ultimately a decision made with the treating team for the individual patient.

Clinical trials#

Every regimen in the treatment section earned its place through clinical trials. Understanding how those trials are built — and what their headline numbers do and don’t prove — is what lets you read the evidence behind a standard of care rather than take it on faith.

How to read a myeloma trial#

New drugs are tested in a standard sequence of phases: phase 1 asks whether a drug is safe and finds the dose, phase 2 looks for early signs it works, phase 3 is the decisive test that randomises patients between the new approach and the current standard so the two can be compared fairly, and phase 4 follows the drug after approval. The trials that change practice are almost all randomised phase 3.

What a trial measures — its endpoint — matters as much as the drug:

  • Overall survival (OS) — do patients live longer? This is the gold standard, but in a disease where people can live many years and receive several later treatments, it takes a very long time to read, and those later therapies blur the signal.
  • Progression-free survival (PFS) — how long until the disease starts growing again? Much faster to measure than OS, and the workhorse endpoint of myeloma trials.
  • Response depth — what fraction reach a complete response, and, deeper still, MRD-negativity (no myeloma detectable at one cell in 100,000 to a million). This is the newest and most sensitive readout.

Trials usually summarise the difference between the two arms as a hazard ratio (HR) — the rate of the bad event (progression, or death) on the new treatment relative to the control. An HR below 1 favours the new treatment: 0.5 means the risk is roughly halved, 0.42 means cut by nearly 60%. The HR captures relative benefit, which is why it is worth reading alongside the absolute figures — the median PFS in months, or the percentage still in remission at a given time — since a large relative reduction on a small baseline can be less impressive than it sounds.

PFS and MRD are surrogate endpoints — stand-ins for the thing we actually care about, living longer. They usually track with survival, but not always, and that gap is the central pitfall in reading these trials. The transplant studies are the clearest example: both IFM 2009 and DETERMINATION showed that adding a transplant substantially improves PFS, yet neither showed an overall-survival benefit — partly because patients who defer transplant can still have one (and other effective drugs) at relapse. A deeper remission is not automatically a longer life. MRD-negativity is now promising enough that regulators accept it in some settings, but it remains a surrogate, not a guarantee.

So the questions worth asking of the trial behind any new approval: What was it compared against — a genuine current standard, or a weaker one? Who were the patients — newly diagnosed and transplant-eligible, or heavily pre-treated? Which endpoint improved — survival, or a surrogate? How large was the benefit in absolute terms, and for how long were patients followed? And was there a control arm at all — many CAR-T and bispecific approvals rest on single-arm response rates, which are striking but have no comparator.

What validates today’s standard of care#

The frontline standard — Dara-VRd, an autologous transplant, and lenalidomide-based maintenance — was assembled one trial at a time. SWOG S0777 established the VRd triplet over Rd; IFM 2009 and DETERMINATION showed that adding a transplant deepens and prolongs remission; a meta-analysis of maintenance trials showed that lenalidomide maintenance afterwards extends both PFS and survival; and GRIFFIN then PERSEUS showed that adding daratumumab to make the quadruplet drives the deepest responses and the longest PFS yet. For patients who cannot have a transplant, MAIA and ALCYONE — and more recently IMROZ and CEPHEUS — built the equivalent daratumumab- or isatuximab-based standard.

The open questions#

The tables above are the settled evidence; the field’s live debates show up in the trials still running or only just reported — and, fittingly, they turn on the surrogate-endpoint problem from earlier.

The sharpest is whether the transplant is still needed now that quadruplets push so many patients to MRD-negativity. MIDAS 20 tested it directly: everyone received an isatuximab-based quadruplet induction, and those already MRD-negative were randomised to have a transplant or not. Adding the transplant did not deepen the response (both arms roughly 85% MRD-negative) — a provocative result, but exactly the place to remember the earlier caveat: MRD-negativity is a surrogate, and the mature progression-free and overall-survival data are not yet in.

A second question is whether frontline CAR-T could replace the transplant altogether — the subject of CARTITUDE-6 21, a head-to-head trial of cilta-cel versus autologous transplant in newly diagnosed patients, which has not yet reported.

A third is how little treatment a patient in deep remission actually needs: MASTER 22 used MRD to guide stopping therapy, and many patients reached a treatment-free phase (though the highest-risk genetics tended to relapse quickly once off it), while DRAMMATIC 23 is the large randomised test of MRD-guided maintenance discontinuation.

The direction of travel is clear: immunotherapy moving earlier, and MRD used not only to measure success but to decide how much treatment each patient needs — possibly, for the first time, less of it.
