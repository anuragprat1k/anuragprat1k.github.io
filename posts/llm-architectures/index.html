The Architectural Progression in Decoder-only LLMs

Going from GPT-2 to Llama-2 to Mixtral, using FLOP-matched compute on TinyStories.
April 9, 2026

The dense backbone from Llama-2 is the de facto standard for modern LLMs, and Mixtral-style sparse MoE is the dominant paradigm at open-weight frontier scale. I wanted to implement both, and their predecessor, GPT-2 and see how the architectural differences between each contribute to a toy task — pretraining on TinyStories at FLOP-matched compute.

Setup#

Data. TinyStories — ~2M short stories generated by GPT-3.5/4, designed so a small model can learn coherent English.

Tokenizer. GPT-2’s BPE (vocab 50257).

FLOP-matched training. Comparing architectures at matched parameter counts is the wrong axis. Every architecture has its own scaling curve — loss as a function of parameter count — and architectural improvements exist precisely to push that curve downward. So fixing params hands the win to whichever architecture is more param-efficient, by definition. The honest axis is compute. Following Chinchilla:

  • Compute-optimal tokens: $D = 20 \cdot N$
  • Compute-optimal FLOPs: $C = 6 \cdot N \cdot D = 120 \cdot N^2$

where $N$ is non-embedding param count. Every run is budgeted to $C = 2 \times 10^{15}$ FLOPs at $N \approx 9.44\text{M}$ active non-embed params — ~8,600 steps at batch=8, seq=512.

Metrics. Train/val loss, val top-1/top-5, wall clock, plus qualitative generation on “Once upon a time” every 1000 steps as a sanity check.

GPT-2: the baseline#

Decoder-only transformer, learned positional embeddings, pre-norm LayerNorm, MHA with full causal attention, GELU MLP, weight-tied input/output embeddings. The architecture that made scaling laws legible.

One interesting bug worth mentioning. I set tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token, then passed ignore_index=pad_token_id into cross_entropy. That masked every EOS — including the legitimate one at the end of each story — out of the loss. The model had no signal to ever stop, and generated until it hit the context limit. Loss curves looked healthy throughout. Qualitative sampling is what caught it; without that I’d have shipped a model that couldn’t stop talking. Fix: mask only the trailing pads, keep the first EOS as a learnable target.

Llama-2: four focused deltas#

Same decoder-only shape as GPT-2, four architectural changes plus three simplifications (no bias, no dropout, no weight tying).

GQA instead of MHA. n_kv_heads < n_heads with grouped sharing. Same expressivity, smaller KV cache — the bottleneck at inference.

RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm. Drops the mean-centering step. Empirically as good, cheaper.

SwiGLU instead of GELU. GELU is a pointwise nonlinearity on a single projection. SwiGLU is gated — two parallel projections, one Swish-activated, multiplied elementwise, then projected:

$$\text{SwiGLU}(x) = W_\text{out}\big(\text{Swish}(W_\text{gate} x) \odot W_\text{up} x\big)$$

Two up-projections give the gate path more capacity; the multiplicative gating lets the MLP suppress or amplify features conditionally. To keep params matched vs GELU’s 4·d hidden, SwiGLU uses 8/3·d.

RoPE instead of learned positional embeddings. The interesting question is where position enters. Attention computes $\text{softmax}(q_i \cdot k_j / \sqrt{d})$ — that dot product is the entire mechanism by which position matters. Rotating $q$ and $k$ by angles proportional to their positions makes the dot product a function of the relative position $(i - j)$ exactly, for free:

$$\langle R_i q, R_j k \rangle = \langle q, R_{j-i} k \rangle$$

Rotating input tokens once would also inject position, but the rotation gets mangled by every subsequent linear projection and you lose the clean relative-position property at attention time. Applying the rotation directly to $q$ and $k$ at every layer, just before the dot product, puts the inductive bias exactly where it’s consumed.

At matched scale, Llama-2 reaches val/loss 1.39 vs GPT-2’s 1.55 — with fewer parameters (9.4M vs 10.6M). The MLP is a wash by construction (the 8/3·d factor in SwiGLU is chosen to match GELU’s param count); the savings come from GQA shrinking the KV projections.

Mixtral: sparse MoE on top of Llama-2#

Mixtral keeps everything from Llama-2 (RMSNorm, RoPE, GQA, SwiGLU) and replaces the single dense FFN per block with a sparse Mixture-of-Experts layer. The bet: scale total parameters without scaling per-token compute, by activating only top_k of N experts per token.

Four pieces, in order:

  1. Router — nn.Linear(d_model, n_experts, bias=False) projects each token into routing logits.
  2. Top-k selection — softmax over experts, then topk(k) (k=2, N=4, matching Mixtral’s shape). Weights renormalized to sum to 1.
  3. Dispatch + compute — each expert is its own SwiGLU MLP. For each expert, gather its assigned tokens, run the expert once, scatter outputs weighted by the router weight. A Python loop here — real scale uses grouped-matmul kernels or expert parallelism.
  4. Aux load-balancing loss — the load-bearing piece.

Why the aux loss matters. Naive MoE collapses within a few hundred steps: the router concentrates tokens on a few popular experts, the rest receive no gradient and stay dead. You lose the capacity you were paying parameters for. The Switch-style penalty:

$$L_\text{aux} = \alpha \cdot N \cdot \sum_i f_i \cdot P_i$$

where $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens routed to expert $i$ (counting each of the k slots, so $\sum_i f_i = k$) and $P_i$ is its mean router probability. The product is minimized when both are uniform. I used $\alpha = 0.01$. Sanity check at init: uniform routing gives $L_\text{aux} \approx k = 2$. First-step log: 2.02.

Active vs total params. The $6N \cdot \text{tokens}$ FLOP approximation needs active params, not total:

$$N_\text{active} = N_\text{non-expert} + \frac{\text{top-}k}{N_\text{experts}} \cdot N_\text{expert}$$

For my config: total non-embed 16.5M, active 9.45M — same compute budget as dense Llama-2. Budget off total params and MoE gets half the steps and underperforms by construction.

Results#

All three at matched compute: $2 \times 10^{15}$ FLOPs, ~9.44M active non-embed params, 6 layers, d_model=384, batch=8, seq=512.

model active total non-embed val/loss val/ppl top-1 top-5 wall clock
GPT-2 9.44M 9.44M 2.302 9.99 49.6% 74.7% 25 min
Llama-2 9.44M 9.44M 1.915 6.79 55.6% 80.6% 30 min
MoE 9.45M 16.5M 1.860 6.42 56.8% 81.9% 52 min

Ranking at matched compute: MoE > Llama-2 > GPT-2, consistent across val/loss, perplexity, and top-k. MoE wins 0.055 nats over Llama-2, 0.44 nats over GPT-2.

The MoE’s advantage over Llama-2 is purchased with parameters, not compute. The 0.055-nat improvement corresponds to a 1.75× increase in total non-embedding parameter count (16.5M vs 9.44M), with active-parameter compute held fixed by construction. Sparse models trade parameter efficiency for compute efficiency, and whether that trade pays off depends on which resource is scarce. At 10M scale, the extra 7M parameters buy roughly one architectural-step’s worth of quality — for reference, SwiGLU alone contributed -0.086 nats in a separate Llama-2 ablation at the same FLOP budget. At 100B+ scale, where parameters are cheap relative to compute, the same trade becomes a substantial win — hence Mixtral 8x7B.

Wall-clock time does not reflect the FLOP accounting. The MoE run takes 52 minutes against 30 minutes for dense Llama-2, a factor of 1.7× despite matched active-parameter compute. The cause is implementation: my expert forward pass is a Python loop running the four experts sequentially, which costs 0.48 ms/step on MPS vs 0.29 ms for the dense MLP. Production MoE avoids this with grouped-matmul kernels or expert parallelism. At this scale the compute advantage of MoE is theoretical — present in FLOPs, not in wall clock.

Load balancing held throughout training. The final auxiliary loss was 2.01, at the uniform-routing floor for top_k=2, and per-expert token allocation stayed balanced from initialization onward. The interpretation is not that the aux loss was unnecessary because routing happened to stay balanced — routing stayed balanced because the aux loss was doing its job. In a separate run with it disabled, the router collapses onto two experts within ~500 steps, leaving the other two as dead parameters for the rest of training.

Generation quality tracks validation loss in the expected direction. All three models converge toward the characteristic TinyStories attractor — stories beginning with “there was a little girl named Lily” — which is a corpus artifact, not an architectural property. At step 8000 the qualitative differences are nonetheless clear: GPT-2 begins repeating itself within two sentences, Llama-2 is cleaner but eventually loops similarly, and the MoE holds the longest coherent span before its own loops begin. Full samples in the appendix.

Takeaways#

Three bullets for the mental model:

  1. “MoE scales capacity without scaling compute” is exactly true in FLOPs, approximately true in systems — dispatch overhead, expert underutilization, memory bandwidth, and comms in expert-parallel setups all eat into the advantage.
  2. Load balancing is the core engineering problem, not the quality headline. The aux loss is a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have.
  3. The param-for-compute trade only pays off when compute is the bottleneck, which is the regime above ~10B dense params. Below that, dense wins on param efficiency.

The four-year progression in one sentence: GPT-2 → Llama-2 is four targeted deltas (GQA, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) that move the param-loss curve outward at every scale; Llama-2 → Mixtral is one structural change (dense FFN → sparse MoE) that only pays above a scale threshold — and only if you solve load balancing.
