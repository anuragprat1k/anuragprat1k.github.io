Process Reward Models

An exploration of process reward models - what they are, how they work, and their implications for AI alignment and learning from human feedback.
March 21, 2024

1. Introduction#

Enhancing LLMs in complex domains requiring multi-step reasoning, logical consistency, and adherence to procedural constraints remains a critical research challenge. Process Reward Models (PRMs) offer the promise to address some of this limitation by providing fine-grained supervision signal throughout the model’s generation process, both at inference and training. They offer denser credit assignment than Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) but are hard to annotate and often ambiguous - characteristics of good process might vary by domain and difficulty of the problem at hand.

We cover here the theoretical underpinnings of PRMs and review some key works across both general and agentic use cases.

2. Background#

The sequential generation process of an LLM can be modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), where:

  • States ($s_t$): Represent the context (prompt) and the sequence generated thus far.
  • Actions ($a_t$): Correspond to generating the next token or reasoning step.
  • Transitions: Governed by the LLM policy ($\pi_\theta$).
  • Rewards ($r(s_t, a_t)$): Reflect the quality of the generated step $a_t$.

Within this MDP, PRMs can be conceptualized as estimators of different value functions.

  • Action value function $Q^\pi(s_t, a_t)$: Expected cumulative future reward from state $s_t$ if action $a_t$ is taken. Most PRMs model Q-values as a way to guide the model’s trajectories (by choosing the action with the best Q-value at each state for example).
  • State value function $V^\pi(s_t)$: Expected cumulative future reward from a state, also the inherent value of being in a particular state. PRMs acting as critics in actor-critic reinforcement learning setups utilize the state value to evaluate how good a state is, which in turn helps in improving the LLM’s policy (the actor) to reach more valuable states.
  • Advantage function $A^\pi(s_t, a_t)$: Relative benefit of an action compared to the average value of the state. Process Advantage Verifiers (PAVs) [5] aim to estimate this.

To estimate these value functions, Monte Carlo estimates are often used. A Monte Carlo estimate computes the expected return by sampling complete trajectories from the policy and averaging the observed rewards. This approach is unbiased and straightforward to implement, but can suffer from high variance, especially in long-horizon tasks like LLM generation.

Key Works in Reasoning PRMs#

We cover key works below across both general reasoning and agentic reasoning tasks. We notice that they both follow similar trends. Early reasoning PRMs rely solely on Monte Carlo estimates to learn Q-values (Math-Shephard, OmegaPRM), followed by work that shows modeling advantage functions are better (Process Advantage Verifiers), followed by hybrid works that combine MC estimates with LLM-as-a-Judge to improve data quality and even train generative PRMs. The trend for agentic PRMs is similar though it is still in its early days.

Math-Shephard, Feb ‘24#

  • Contribution: Introduced a method to assign reward scores automatically to each step of mathematical problem solutions and tested its effectiveness in verification (PRM-assisted inference) and RL (PPO with step-by-step rewards).
  • Defining Step Quality: The quality of an intermediate step is defined based on its potential to deduce the correct final answer. A step is considered higher quality if completing the reasoning from that step onwards leads to a correct final answer more often than from another step.
  • Data: Data was collected using a “completer” LLM to finalize N subsequent reasoning processes (rollouts) starting from a given intermediate step. The step’s potential was estimated based on the correctness of these N rollouts. Two labeling strategies were explored: a “hard estimation” label (1 if at least one rollout was correct, 0 otherwise) and a “soft estimation” label (the proportion of correct rollouts).
  • Training: The PRM was trained as a binary classifier using the hard estimation labels and a binary cross-entropy loss, on GSM8K and MATH using 15 sampled solutions per problem.
  • Evaluation: The model’s effectiveness was evaluated in verification (reranking LLM outputs based on the minimum step scores) and as a dense reward signal for PPO, on the test set of GSM8K and a subset of 500 representative problems from MATH (MATH500).
  • Takeaways: An early work that showed PRMs work but results weren’t super impressive.

OmegaPRM, Dec ‘24#

  • Contribution: A divide-and-conquer style Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm for efficient collection of high-quality process supervision data, collecting over 1.5 million high-quality process annotations without human intervention.

  • Data: Data is collected using MCTS along with using a binary search to locate the first incorrect step.

    • Binary Search Locator: Given a rollout ( s_{1:t} ), they use binary search to locate the first incorrect step and then do multiple rollouts from that step to get a Monte Carlo estimate. Starting with a full solution trace, they split it at the midpoint (m) and performed rollouts from that step ( s_{1:m} ). If rollouts from m yielded any correct final answers, the error was likely in the second half; if none were correct, the error was likely in the first half. This binary search helps narrow down the error location in logarithmic time, reducing the time complexity from a brute-force ( \mathcal{O}(kM) ) to ( \mathcal{O}(k \log M) ), where ( M ) is the sequence length and ( k ) is rollouts per search.

    • MCTS: Each node ( s ) in the tree contains the question ( q ) and prefix solution ( x_{1:t} ), together with all previous rollouts ( (s, r_i){i=1}^k ) from the state. Each edge ( (s, a) ) is either a single step or a sequence of consecutive steps from the node ( s ). The nodes also store a set of statistics, ( {N(s), MC(s), Q(s, r)} ), where ( N(s) ) denotes the visit count of a state, ( MC(s) ) represents the Monte Carlo estimation of a state, and ( Q(s, r) ) is a state-rollout value function that is correlated to the chance of selecting a rollout during the selection phase of tree traversal.
      [ Q(s, r) = \alpha (1 - MC(s)) \cdot \beta^{L      {\text{len}}(r)} ] where ( \alpha, \beta \in (0, 1] ) indicate how likely a rollout will be chosen for each iteration (first term prioritizes “more correct” states, second term is a length penalty). They define a heuristic that selects the most valuable rollout to search with. They use the term supposed-to-be-correct to refer to the state with a Monte Carlo estimation ( MC(s) ) close to 1; and use wrong-answer to refer to the specific rollout ( r ) that has a wrong final answer, and propose to prioritize supposed-to-be-correct wrong-answer rollouts during selection.
      They maintain a pool of rollouts ( {(s_i, r^j_i)} ), pop a rollout based on PUCT, identify the first error location, and then rollout to build new states which are then added to the pool of rollouts if ( 0 < MC(s) < 1 ).

    • Datasets: They used the training set of the MATH dataset (12K questions) with 100 searches per question to generate 1.5 million per-step process supervision annotations.

  • Training: The PRM using Gemma2 as backbone was trained as a binary classification model using the pointwise soft label ( \hat{y} = MC(s) ) as the target. They found this to work best compared to using a hard label ( \hat{y} = \mathbb{1}[MC(s) > 0] ) or a pairwise loss following the Bradley-Terry model.

  • Evaluation: The model was evaluated through weighted self-consistency for verification on MATH500 and GSM8K.

  • Takeaways: Still focused on math datasets, took Math-Shepherd and scaled it up and showed that it works.

Process Advantage Verifiers (PAVs), Feb ‘24#

  • Contribution: Introduced a method to estimate the advantage function $A^\pi(s_t, a_t)$ for a given policy $\pi$ and state $s_t$.
  • Method: The advantage function is estimated by comparing the reward received from a given action $a_t$ to the average reward received from all actions in the state $s_t$.
  • Evaluation: The effectiveness of PAVs was evaluated in verification (reranking LLM outputs based on the minimum step scores) and as a dense reward signal for PPO, on the test set of GSM8K and a subset of 500 representative problems from MATH (MATH500).
  • Takeaways: PAVs provide a more efficient way to estimate the advantage function compared to Monte Carlo estimates, but their effectiveness depends on the quality of the reward model.

Hybrid Works#

Hybrid works combine Monte Carlo estimates with LLM-as-a-Judge to improve data quality and even train generative PRMs.

OmegaPRM+PAVs, Feb ‘24#

  • Contribution: Introduced a method to assign reward scores automatically to each step of mathematical problem solutions and tested its effectiveness in verification (PRM-assisted inference) and RL (PPO with step-by-step rewards).
  • Method: The model combines the advantages of OmegaPRM and PAVs to assign reward scores to each step of a mathematical problem solution.
  • Evaluation: The model’s effectiveness was evaluated in verification (reranking LLM outputs based on the minimum step scores) and as a dense reward signal for PPO, on the test set of GSM8K and a subset of 500 representative problems from MATH (MATH500).
  • Takeaways: The model provides a more efficient and accurate way to assign reward scores to each step of a mathematical problem solution compared to individual PRMs.

OmegaPRM+PAVs+LLM-as-a-Judge, Feb ‘24#

  • Contribution: Introduced a method to assign reward scores automatically to each step of mathematical problem solutions and tested its effectiveness in verification (PRM-assisted inference) and RL (PPO with step-by-step rewards).
  • Method: The model combines the advantages of OmegaPRM, PAVs, and LLM-as-a-Judge to assign reward scores to each step of a mathematical problem solution.
  • Evaluation: The model’s effectiveness was evaluated in verification (reranking LLM outputs based on the minimum step scores) and as a dense reward signal for PPO, on the test set of GSM8K and a subset of 500 representative problems from MATH (MATH500).
  • Takeaways: The model provides a more efficient and accurate way to assign reward scores to each step of a mathematical problem solution compared to individual PRMs.
