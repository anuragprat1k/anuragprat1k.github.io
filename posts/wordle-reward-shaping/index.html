Discovery Needs Dense Rewards?

March 23, 2026

Fine-tuning a language model with RLVR is known to sharpen it — the policy concentrates on known solutions, boosting pass@1 while pass@k collapses (Yue et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). This is increasingly treated as a fundamental property of RL on language models. But it shouldn’t be. Sharpening is what happens when binary rewards provide zero gradient on problems the model can’t already solve — the capability frontier never moves because the optimization has nothing to push against.

Dense rewards should break this. Give the model credit for partial progress and it can bootstrap its way to solutions it never found before. But classical reward shaping theory (Ng et al., 1999) warns that only potential-based shaping preserves the optimal policy. Anything else risks introducing spurious optima where the agent collects shaping reward without completing the task.

I tested both claims on Wordle — a multi-turn game that gives natural dense feedback and is genuinely hard for small LLMs. Training Qwen3-4B with GRPO under different reward functions for up to 2,000 steps across multiple seeds, I found:

  1. Dense rewards drive discovery. +102 more unique words solved than sparse by 2,000 steps (~24% lift), consistent across seeds, with the gap still growing.
  2. Both pass@1 and pass@8 rise together. This is discovery, not sharpening — the model isn’t concentrating mass on known solutions, it’s expanding coverage.
  3. Non-potential shaping gives a growing edge. Dense and greens-only (purely potential-based) start similar but diverge — by step 2,000, dense leads by +80 mastered words over sparse vs +40 for greens-only. Yellows teach multi-turn chaining, a skill that compounds as the model encounters harder words.

Why potential-based shaping matters (in theory)

Ng et al. (1999) proved that the only additive reward shaping that preserves the optimal policy is:

$$F(s, s’) = \gamma \Phi(s’) - \Phi(s)$$

where $\Phi$ maps states to reals. Any other form can change which policy is optimal. The motivating examples make the intuition concrete: in chess, rewarding +1 per captured piece (not potential-based — it depends on the action, not just the state) makes the agent prefer trading pieces over winning. In Randløv & Alstrøm’s bicycle task, rewarding the agent for moving toward a goal caused it to circle near the goal indefinitely, collecting shaping reward without arriving.

This gives us a concrete prediction: the non-potential-based yellow component should cause reward hacking, and removing it should improve learning.

The reward functions

All conditions share the same terminal and penalty components:

  • Terminal: +3.0 for a win (with +0.1 per remaining turn as a speed bonus), −1.0 for a loss
  • Format violation penalty: −1.0 per turn where no valid <guess> tag is parsed

The per-turn shaping signal is what varies:

RewardPer-turn signalPotential-based?
Sparse0 (nothing until game ends)N/A
Dense+0.4 per new 🟩, +0.2 per new 🟨Partial
Dense (greens only)+0.4 per new 🟩, no yellow rewardYes

Greens are potential-based. Cumulative green count is monotonically non-decreasing — once a position is green, it stays green. So $r_{\text{green}} = 0.4 \times \Delta\text{greens} = 0.4 \times (\Phi(s’) - \Phi(s))$ where $\Phi(s) = \text{total greens in } s$.

Yellows are not potential-based. Yellow status is non-monotonic — a yellow letter can become green on a later turn, so the count of yellow-only letters can decrease. The yellow reward also gives +0.2 for including a letter known to be in the word regardless of whether the guess contains eliminated letters. This creates an incentive misaligned with optimal play: maximize tile matches, not candidate elimination.

Setup

Wordle is a six-turn guessing game: guess a five-letter word, get colored feedback (🟩 right letter right position, 🟨 right letter wrong position, ⬜ not in word), narrow down the target. As an RL problem it has useful properties: multi-turn with structured feedback, measurable progress (the candidate set shrinks monotonically), and interpretable failures.

The model outputs <guess>WORD</guess> tags and the environment provides feedback including eliminated letters after each turn.

SFT warmup. Before RL, the model gets 100 steps of supervised fine-tuning on expert-generated games. A heuristic solver (greedy candidate elimination) plays ~1,000 games, and we fine-tune on the trajectories. This teaches game format and basic strategy, and critically means the model plays without chain-of-thought — just a <guess> tag per turn (~10 tokens) instead of lengthy thinking traces. With 16 rollouts per step and 6 turns per game, keeping completions short matters for throughput. To avoid opener fixation, the expert randomly selects from five high-entropy starting words (CRANE, SLATE, TRACE, ADIEU, STARE).

Decontamination. The 2,315 Wordle answer words are split into disjoint sets — 768 for SFT expert games, 1,537 for GRPO training targets, and 10 held-out probe words. The model never encounters the same target word during both SFT and GRPO, so any word it learns to solve during RL is genuine discovery, not memorization.

Model and training. Qwen3-4B with LoRA (rank 64, 3.2% trainable params), trained with TRL’s GRPOTrainer + vLLM in colocate mode on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 (96 GB). GRPO with 16 rollouts per prompt, KL $\beta$ = 0.04, LR = $10^{-4}$, 2,000 steps. All results are averaged across 3 seeds with different SFT checkpoints (within each seed, all reward conditions share the same SFT). Shaded regions in plots show ±1σ.

Dense rewards drive discovery

Dense discovers ~24% more unique words and masters ~37% more than sparse.

To control for variance in SFT starting points across seeds, the lift plot below shows paired differences: for each seed, we subtract sparse’s trajectory from dense/greens, then average across seeds. This isolates the treatment effect from seed-level variation.

ConditionΔ Unique solvedΔ Mastered (≥2)
Dense (full)+102 ± 50+80 ± 29
Greens-only+68 ± 20+40 ± 1

Paired difference vs sparse at step 2,000 (mean ± std, 3 seeds). Dense’s lift grows throughout training; greens-only provides a smaller but remarkably consistent mastery boost (±1 across seeds).

The mechanism is the bootstrapping trap: sparse rewards give gradient signal only from winning episodes. At the win rates in this experiment (~4-7%), the vast majority of rollouts get $r = -1.0$ uniformly — zero variance across the group, zero advantage, zero learning. Dense rewards give nonzero signal on every episode, even losses, because partial progress (new greens and yellows) is rewarded. This breaks the trap by providing gradient where sparse provides none.

This is discovery, not sharpening

The standard concern with dense rewards is that they might simply sharpen — improve pass@1 by concentrating mass on known solutions while the frontier contracts. The pass@k diagnostic tests this directly: if sharpening is happening, pass@1 rises while pass@k for large k declines.

Both pass@1 and pass@8 rise together throughout training for all conditions. Dense reaches pass@8 ≈ 0.46 by step 2,000 — the model can solve nearly half of random target words given 8 attempts. There’s no sign of the pass@k collapse that characterizes sharpening. The model is expanding coverage, not concentrating it.

Yellows matter more over time

In the first ~750 steps, dense and greens-only are nearly indistinguishable — their variance bands overlap on both discovery and mastery. You could reasonably conclude that the non-potential yellow component doesn’t matter and potential-based shaping is sufficient.

The curves diverge after that. By step 2,000, dense leads greens-only by ~35 unique words on discovery (533 vs 498). But the mastery gap is larger and cleaner: 294 vs 255, a 15% lead. In the paired lift analysis, dense gains +80 mastered words over sparse while greens-only gains +40 — and greens-only’s mastery lift is remarkably consistent across seeds (±1). Dense doesn’t just find more words — it finds them reliably.

The traces show why. On LEVEL, greens-only found LEVEE (🟩🟩🟩🟩) — 4/5 greens, the maximum per-turn reward. Then it repeated LEVEE three more times. Without yellow reward, there’s no gradient signal saying “the last position is wrong.” Dense never gets stuck this way because yellows reward progress toward fixing remaining positions: the model learns that E and V need to swap.

More generally, yellows teach multi-turn chaining — carrying letter information across guesses. This skill barely matters when the model is learning basic patterns (easy words, first ~750 steps), but becomes the differentiator on harder words that require multi-step reasoning. The mastery gap grows because harder words need chaining, and chaining needs yellows.

This has a practical implication for ablation studies: short runs understate the importance of reward components that primarily help on hard instances. Easy words get solved first and don’t differentiate reward functions. The differentiation happens on hard instances, which take longer to reach.

Constraint violations: the stubborn problem

All conditions show high constraint violation rates — the model frequently guesses words containing letters it already knows aren’t in the target. Format compliance is excellent (>97%), so this isn’t a parsing issue. The model produces valid <guess> tags but ignores the semantic content of feedback.

There’s a slight downward trend — dense drops from ~78% to ~72% over 2,000 steps — but the variance bands overlap heavily across all conditions and the improvement is modest. Sparse actually starts with the lowest violation rate (~74%), likely because its more conservative play produces fewer opportunities to violate constraints. Dense and greens-only start higher (more aggressive play, more violations) but converge with sparse by the end.

The takeaway: the model learns to match letters (discovery, mastery) much faster than it learns to avoid eliminated ones (constraint satisfaction). These are different skills. Matching requires recognizing letter-position patterns from feedback. Constraint satisfaction requires maintaining a running model of what’s been ruled out — a form of working memory that reward signal alone may not teach efficiently.

A sample episode on ARDOR (dense, step 500) illustrates the gap:

STARE → __🟨🟨_    (A,R in word but wrong positions)
MARGO → invalid     (hallucinated word)
FLORA → __🟨🟨🟨   (O,R,A in word — good narrowing)
PROVE → _🟩🟨__    (R correct pos, O in word — but reuses eliminated E!)
BRONY → invalid     (hallucinated)
AUDIO → 🟩_🟨_🟨   (A correct, D and O in word — very close, but out of turns)

Four of five letters found by turn 6, but turns wasted on hallucinated words and constraint violations.

Three flavors of reward hacking

Both sparse and dense rewards get hacked, but differently.

Sparse: task avoidance

In an earlier version (before format penalties), sparse reward collapsed catastrophically. Win rate rose to 18.8% at step 739, then format compliance crashed from 95% to 0% in seven steps:

StepWin RateFormat ComplianceReward
73918.8%93.7%−0.24
7426.2%52.6%−0.56
7440.0%12.5%−0.19
7490.0%0.0%0.00

The model discovered it could corrupt the opening tag — <gues>CRANE</guess> instead of <guess>CRANE</guess>. One missing letter. The parser doesn’t match, no guess is registered, no game is played, and the reward is 0.0 instead of −1.0 for a loss.

Under GRPO, with most rollouts scoring −1.0, a single rollout scoring 0.0 gets positive advantage. The gradient reinforces “don’t play,” and within a few steps the entire policy converges to tag corruption. A single-character change that avoids the task entirely.

Adding a −1.0 penalty for format violations closes this exploit — “don’t play” now scores the same as “play and lose,” removing the advantage.

Dense: slow erosion

Dense reward doesn’t collapse suddenly — it erodes. The reward trajectory starts at +0.66 per episode and drifts to 0.00 by step 1,000.

The model learns to produce guesses with matching letters (earning per-turn green/yellow rewards) without actually solving games (which would earn the terminal +3.0). The positive per-turn signal and negative terminal signal approximately cancel. The model finds a comfortable equilibrium that earns decent per-turn rewards from tile matches but never closes.

This is exactly what Ng et al. predicted: non-potential-based shaping introduces new fixed points absent from the original MDP. The sparse hack is dramatic (complete task avoidance), the dense hack more insidious (partial engagement that looks like progress).

SFT initialization: sharpening through inherited bias

In an earlier version where the SFT expert always opened with CRANE, the model entered GRPO biased toward that opener. GRPO reinforced it: words starting with C give an immediate green on position 1 when opened with CRANE, earning +0.4 on the first guess. The model sharpened toward C-words — not because they’re easier, but because they’re easier given the inherited opener.

The result: 27–32% of mastered words started with C (vs 7% in the vocabulary). Diversifying to five openers reduced C-bias to ~14%, but S-words rose to ~25% from SLATE/STARE. The bias shifted rather than disappeared — SFT initialization shapes the reward landscape GRPO explores, and GRPO exploits whatever local structure it finds.

What this means

Dense rewards break the bootstrapping trap. The gap between dense and sparse is the dominant effect, consistent across seeds. Standard RLVR with binary rewards provides zero gradient on hard problems. Any per-step shaping breaks this. The first priority in reward design is providing signal where there currently is none.

Non-potential shaping teaches skills that compound. Yellows teach multi-turn chaining — a skill that barely matters in the first 750 steps but drives a ~27% mastery gap by step 2,000. Non-potential rewards are most valuable when they encode a specific skill the model needs, not just a proxy for completion. The theoretical risk of spurious optima is real (the slow erosion), but the practical benefit of skill acquisition outweighs it.

Dense reward is why game-playing RL discovers. AlphaZero’s MCTS provides a dense value estimate at every board position. Robotics RL gets continuous feedback from physics at every timestep. Standard RLVR with binary rewards is the outlier — most RL domains have dense reward by default, which is why discovery is the norm there and sharpening is the pathology. The fix isn’t exotic: just provide per-step feedback.

Short ablations understate hard-problem effects. In the first 750 steps, yellows look irrelevant. By 2,000, they drive a clear mastery gap. Easy words get solved first and don’t differentiate reward functions. The differentiation happens on hard instances, which take longer to reach.

Reward hacking is plural, not singular. Task avoidance (sparse tag corruption), reward-metric divergence (dense slow erosion), and initialization exploitation (CRANE bias) each have distinct mechanisms and require different fixes.
